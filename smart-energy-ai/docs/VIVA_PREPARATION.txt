Q1: What is the title and core objective of your project?
- Answer: The project is titled "AI Agent for Smart Energy Management" (SmartEnergy AI). Its objective is to provide an autonomous multi-agent decision support platform that analyzes smart-meter data, forecasts demand, detects anomalies, and optimizes electricity expenses.
- Simple explanation: It is like an automated personal energy expert that studies your power usage and cuts your electricity bills.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why is smart energy management important in current power systems?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "With the rise of renewable energy integration and Time-of-Day pricing, load profile management and peak-shaving are critical to reducing grid stress and consumer electricity bills."

Q2: How does your system work from a user's perspective?
- Answer: The user loads smart-meter data via CSV or demo generation, configures tariff rates, and receives comprehensive analysis across 10 interactive dashboard tabs, complete with an AI assistant and downloadable audit report.
- Simple explanation: Upload data, click Analyze, view graphs and money-saving schedules, and ask questions in a chat window.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Does the user need to understand machine learning to use this?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "No. The system abstracts data science complexity behind intuitive KPI metric cards, Plotly charts, and plain English summaries synthesized by the Coordinator Agent."

Q3: What kind of dataset does this system take as input?
- Answer: Time-series smart-meter data containing at minimum chronological timestamps and hourly consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh).
- Simple explanation: A spreadsheet showing the date, hour, and how much electricity was consumed each hour.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What additional features are supported if available?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "The pipeline optionally consumes active power (kW), voltage (V), ambient temperature (°C), and appliance-level sub-metering attributes."

Q4: What units are used to measure electricity in your project?
- Answer: Power is measured in kilowatts (kW) and energy consumption is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). One kWh equals one unit of electricity on an Indian utility bill.
- Simple explanation: kW is the speed of electricity consumption right now; kWh is the total amount consumed over time.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What is the mathematical relationship between kW and kWh?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Energy (kWh) is the integral of active power P(t) (kW) with respect to time (hours): Energy = ∫ P(t) dt. For discrete 1-hour smart-meter intervals, kWh numerically equals average kW."

Q5: What currency and tariff structure is used as default?
- Answer: Indian Rupees (INR ₹) using a Time-of-Day (ToD) tariff structure: standard rate ₹7.50/kWh, peak rate ₹11.00/kWh, off-peak rate ₹5.20/kWh, and fixed base charge ₹150/month.
- Simple explanation: Indian electricity pricing where night power is cheap and evening power is expensive.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Is this tariff fixed or configurable?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "It is fully parameterized via `TariffConfig` in `utils/config.py` and can be adjusted interactively in Tab 2 of the Gradio dashboard."

Q6: What is the difference between peak and off-peak hours?
- Answer: Peak hours (18:00 to 22:00) represent high system-wide grid demand when utilities charge premium rates. Off-peak hours (23:00 to 06:00) represent nighttime baseload periods with discounted rates.
- Simple explanation: Peak hours are when everyone is home cooking and turning on ACs; off-peak is when most people are asleep.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why do utilities charge higher rates during peak hours?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "To reflect higher marginal generation costs from peaker plants and economically incentivize consumers to shift flexible demand."

Q7: Does this software directly switch physical circuit breakers?
- Answer: No. SmartEnergy AI is strictly an advisory and decision-support system. It does not interface with high-voltage relays or breakers.
- Simple explanation: It gives intelligent recommendations to the user; it does not touch the home's physical wiring.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why not automate physical switching?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Direct actuation requires certified industrial IoT hardware, safety interlocks, and fail-safe relays outside the scope of software simulation."

Q8: What is an AI agent?

- Simple explanation: An automated digital specialist with a clear job, tools, and decision-making logic.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How does an agent differ from a standard Python script?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "A standard script follows rigid sequential logic; an agent maintains internal state, evaluates context, invokes tools conditionally, and can employ LLM reasoning for non-deterministic synthesis."

Q9: What is the difference between an AI agent and a chatbot?
- Answer: A chatbot simply predicts text responses to prompts. An AI agent possesses goal-directed autonomy, structured state, access to deterministic tools and ML models, and workflow coordination.
- Simple explanation: A chatbot just chats; an AI agent actually does analytical work and calls mathematical tools.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Can a chatbot be part of an agent system?"

Q10: Why did you use six agents instead of just one single LLM?

- Simple explanation: One person cannot do every job in an office perfectly; specialists prevent mistakes.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What happens if a single LLM tries to do all these tasks?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "LLMs are statistical next-token predictors. When tasked with arithmetic, ML training, and multi-tier tariff calculations simultaneously, they frequently hallucinate incorrect numbers."

CATEGORY 3: MACHINE LEARNING & FORECASTING (18 to 27)
================================================================================

Q18: What machine learning model is used for forecasting?
- Answer: A Random Forest Regressor (`sklearn.ensemble.RandomForestRegressor`).
- Simple explanation: An ensemble of many decision trees that vote together to predict future electricity usage.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why choose Random Forest over ARIMA or LSTM?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Random Forest non-linearly captures diurnal peaks and temperature interactions, requires zero stationarity differencing unlike ARIMA, and trains in under 2 seconds on standard CPU hardware without deep learning dependencies."

Q19: What features are fed into the forecasting model?
- Answer:
  1. `hour` (Hour of day: 0 to 23)
  2. `is_weekend` (Binary: 0 or 1)
  3. `lag_1` (Energy consumption 1 hour prior)
  4. `lag_2` (Energy consumption 2 hours prior)
  5. `lag_24` (Energy consumption at the same hour yesterday)
  6. `roll_mean_6` (6-hour rolling moving average)
  7. `roll_mean_24` (24-hour diurnal rolling moving average)
  8. `temperature` (Ambient temperature in °C, if present)
- Simple explanation: What time it is, whether it's a weekend, recent usage, and yesterday's usage at this exact hour.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why are lag_1 and lag_24 particularly important?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "`lag_1` captures short-term autoregressive persistence, while `lag_24` captures strong 24-hour diurnal cyclical periodicity."

Q20: How did you split your data for training and testing?
- Answer: We use a chronological 80/20 train/test split.
- Simple explanation: We train on the first 80% of days and test on the final 20% of days.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why not use standard k-fold random shuffle cross-validation?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Random shuffling causes 'data leakage' in time series, where future records leak into training sets. Chronological splitting preserves temporal causality."

Q21: What evaluation metrics did you calculate for the forecast?
- Answer: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE).
- Simple explanation: Standard mathematical formulas that measure how close our predictions were to actual historical data.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What were your typical measured metric values?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "On our 45-day dataset, the model achieves an MAE of approximately 0.18 kWh, an RMSE of 0.24 kWh, and a MAPE of ~11.5%."

Q22: What is MAE (Mean Absolute Error)?
- Answer: MAE is the average magnitude of absolute errors between predicted and actual values: MAE = (1/n) * Σ |y_actual - y_pred|.
- Simple explanation: On average, how many kilowatt-hours our prediction was off by.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What is an advantage of MAE over RMSE?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "MAE is robust to outliers and expresses errors directly in the original physical units (kWh) without quadratic penalization."

Q23: What is RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)?
- Answer: RMSE is the square root of the mean squared errors: RMSE = sqrt((1/n) * Σ (y_actual - y_pred)²).
- Simple explanation: An error metric that penalizes large mistakes much more heavily than small mistakes.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why is RMSE almost always higher than MAE?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Because squaring the residuals magnifies larger deviations, making RMSE sensitive to sudden anomalous spikes."

Q24: What is MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error)?
- Answer: MAPE measures the percentage error relative to the actual value: MAPE = (100%/n) * Σ |(y_actual - y_pred) / y_actual|.
- Simple explanation: The prediction error expressed as a percentage of actual consumption.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What problem occurs in MAPE when actual consumption is zero?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Division by zero occurs. In `services/forecasting.py`, we implement a numerical safety guard: `y_true_safe = np.maximum(y_true, 0.05)` to guarantee stability."

Q25: What is the forecast horizon?
- Answer: 24 hours (1 full day ahead) at 1-hour resolution.
- Simple explanation: We predict every hour from now until the same time tomorrow.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How does the model predict step 12 when real lag_1 is not available yet?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "We implement recursive multi-step autoregressive forecasting, feeding predicted outputs from earlier steps back into the feature buffer for subsequent steps."

Q26: What hyperparameters did you choose for the Random Forest?
- Answer: `n_estimators=75`, `max_depth=10`, `random_state=42`, and `n_jobs=1`.
- Simple explanation: 75 decision trees with a maximum depth of 10 levels to prevent overfitting.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why limit max_depth to 10?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Unconstrained trees can memorize noise. A depth of 10 bounds model complexity while preserving rapid millisecond inference on desktop CPUs."

Q27: Can your model predict weather-driven seasonal changes?
- Answer: The architecture accepts ambient temperature as an exogenous feature. Given summer or winter historical records, the model automatically correlates thermal variation with cooling or heating demand.
- Simple explanation: Yes, if temperature data is provided, it learns that hot afternoons mean higher AC usage.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How would you handle full multi-year seasonality?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "By adding harmonic Fourier terms (sine/cosine transforms) for annual cycles (Day of Year) into the feature matrix."

CATEGORY 4: ANOMALY DETECTION & SAFETY (28 to 36)
================================================================================

Q28: What is anomaly detection in the context of smart meters?
- Answer: Anomaly detection is the identification of data points or consumption profiles that deviate significantly from expected normal behavioral patterns.
- Simple explanation: Spotting weird, unexpected electricity usage, like high power at 3 AM.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What are the common causes of smart meter anomalies?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Appliances inadvertently left operating, insulation degradation, sudden concurrent appliance operation, meter calibration drift, or grid voltage sags."

Q29: What algorithm did you use for anomaly detection?
- Answer: A hybrid approach combining Hour-Specific Diurnal Z-Score thresholding (Z >= 2.8) with scikit-learn's Isolation Forest algorithm (contamination=3%).
- Simple explanation: We compare each hour's reading against normal readings for THAT exact time of day, plus an Isolation Forest model.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why is an hour-specific diurnal Z-score better than a global Z-score?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "A consumption of 3.0 kWh might be completely normal at 8:00 PM during dinner, but an extreme anomaly at 3:00 AM. Global Z-scores miss time-dependent anomalies entirely."

Q30: How does the Isolation Forest algorithm work?
- Answer: Isolation Forest isolates anomalies by randomly partitioning feature space using decision trees. Anomalous instances require fewer random splits to isolate and thus exhibit significantly shorter tree path lengths.
- Simple explanation: Weird points are few and different, so they get separated in very few questions.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What features are passed into Isolation Forest?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "`energy_kwh`, `hour`, `is_weekend`, and optionally `temperature`."

Q31: What is a Z-score?
- Answer: The Z-score measures how many standard deviations an observation x is from the mean μ: Z = (x - μ) / σ.
- Simple explanation: A score showing how far a number is from average. A score above 3 is extremely rare and abnormal.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What Z-score threshold did you set?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "We set 2.8 for general anomalies and 3.5 for 'Critical' severity classifications."

Q32: How do you categorize anomaly severity?
- Answer:
  - Critical: Z >= 3.5 (Severe sudden spike)
  - High: Z >= 2.5
  - Medium: Z >= 1.8 or Isolation Forest outlier flag
  - Low: Minor statistical deviations
- Simple explanation: Low, Medium, High, and Critical based on how extreme the spike is.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How are late-night anomalies specifically handled?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Events occurring between 00:00 and 05:00 with Z > 1.8 are specifically highlighted as potential unattended appliance energy leaks."

Q33: Can this system diagnose an electrical short-circuit or physical fault?
- Answer: No. The system strictly detects behavioral data anomalies. It explicitly warns that anomalies do not constitute proof of hardware faults.
- Simple explanation: It knows the power was high, but it cannot see inside your walls.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why is this disclaimer legally and ethically important?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Directing users to inspect breakers or wiring without certified electrician qualifications creates electrocution and liability hazards. Our system recommends safe appliance checks only."

Q34: What voltage anomalies does the system recognize?
- Answer: If a voltage channel is provided, readings dropping below 215V during heavy load spikes are annotated as low grid voltage conditions.
- Simple explanation: It spots voltage dips caused by excessive heavy equipment starting up.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What physical phenomenon causes voltage drop during spikes?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Ohmic resistance drop along service conductors (I*R drop) during high instantaneous current draws."

Q35: What is the false positive rate of your anomaly detector?
- Answer: By setting contamination to 3% and Z-score threshold to 2.8, the system restricts false positives to approximately 2% to 3% of baseline readings.
- Simple explanation: About 2 or 3 out of every 100 readings are flagged, catching real issues without annoying the user.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How could you reduce false positives further?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "By incorporating user feedback loops where users mark flagged events as 'intended usage' to adjust user-specific priors."

Q36: What safe troubleshooting actions does Agent 5 recommend?
- Answer: Inspecting whether air conditioners, heaters, or induction cooktops were inadvertently left running, or verifying appliance timer settings.
- Simple explanation: Check if someone forgot to turn off the AC or geyser.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What does it explicitly tell the user NOT to do?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "It explicitly instructs users never to dismantle electrical panels, touch distribution breakers, or tamper with meter seals."

Q37: What is the difference between flexible and non-flexible appliances?
- Answer: Flexible (deferrable) appliances have operational times that can be rescheduled without compromising user comfort (e.g. washing machines, EV charging, pool pumps, water heaters). Non-flexible appliances have fixed on-demand duty cycles (e.g. refrigerators, medical equipment, active lighting).
- Simple explanation: Flexible appliances can be run at night; inflexible ones (like a fridge) must run constantly.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How does the system treat semi-flexible loads like Air Conditioners?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "ACs cannot be turned off entirely, but their thermal setpoints can be modulated by 1°C to 2°C to shave peak wattage."

Q38: What is "Load Shifting"?
- Answer: Load shifting is a demand-side management technique where electricity consumption is rescheduled from expensive peak-tariff hours to discounted off-peak hours.
- Simple explanation: Moving your power use from expensive times to cheap times.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Does load shifting reduce total energy consumed or just cost?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Primarily cost, by exploiting time-varying tariff differentials. However, accompanying efficiency tips (like eco-modes) also reduce gross kWh."

Q39: How are appliance monthly electricity costs calculated?
- Answer: Monthly kWh = Rated Power (kW) * Typical Daily Hours * 30 days. Cost = Monthly kWh * Blended Tariff Rate.
- Simple explanation: Power times hours times 30 days times price per unit.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How is the blended tariff rate calculated?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Unmanaged usage assumes 45% peak rate and 55% base rate; managed usage shifts 80% of flexible energy to off-peak tariff rates."

Q40: How much money does the system typically save?
- Answer: Depending on the proportion of flexible appliances and peak tariff differentials, projected savings typically range between 15% and 22% of the monthly electricity bill.
- Simple explanation: About 15% to 22% off your monthly bill.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Can you guarantee these savings?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "No. All financial projections are analytical estimates based on simulation and tariffs. Actual bills depend on user adherence, utility surcharges, and weather."

Q41: What is a Time-of-Day (ToD) tariff?
- Answer: A pricing scheme where electricity rates vary according to the hour of the day to reflect supply and demand dynamics on the power grid.
- Simple explanation: Electricity costs more when everyone is using it (evening) and less when nobody is using it (night).
- If examiner asks deeper: "Are ToD tariffs prevalent in India?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Yes, mandated by state regulatory commissions (SERCs) for commercial, industrial, and increasingly high-consumption residential consumers equipped with smart meters."

Q42: What are tiered slab rates?
- Answer: A progressive tariff structure where the cost per kWh increases as cumulative monthly consumption crosses specific thresholds (e.g., 0-100 kWh @ ₹4.50, 101-300 kWh @ ₹7.50, >300 kWh @ ₹10.00).
- Simple explanation: The more electricity you use overall in a month, the more expensive each unit becomes.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Does your system model slab rates?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Yes. `calculate_energy_costs()` in `services/optimization.py` models both ToD and tiered slab brackets."

Q43: Which appliance is usually the largest energy consumer in residential homes?
- Answer: Air Conditioning (HVAC) systems and electric water heaters (geysers). A 1.5 Ton AC consumes ~1.6 kW, accounting for 35% to 50% of typical summer consumption.
- Simple explanation: Air conditioners and water geysers.
- If examiner asks deeper: "What efficiency advice is given for ACs?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Setting the thermostat to 24°C–25°C. Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) data confirms that each 1°C increase saves ~6% electricity."

Q44: What is vampire or standby power?
- Answer: Electric power consumed by appliances while they are switched off or in standby mode (e.g. TVs, microwave displays, laptop chargers, set-top boxes).
- Simple explanation: Devices silently sipping electricity even when you are not using them.
- If examiner asks deeper: "How much energy does standby power waste?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Typically 3% to 8% of residential electricity consumption."

Q45: How does Agent 3 compute savings for an inflexible appliance?
- Answer: By applying conservative standby reduction factors (e.g. 3% reduction via smart plug elimination of idle vampire load) without altering core operating hours.
- Simple explanation: It doesn't move the fridge hours; it just suggests eliminating idle waste.
- If examiner asks deeper: "Why not schedule the refrigerator to turn off during peak hours?"
- Suggested deeper answer: "Turning off refrigeration compromises food safety and causes temperature rebound spikes that draw extra power upon restart."
